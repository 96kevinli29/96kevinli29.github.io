Beyond GDP

GDP per person tells us how much a country produces in a year. It does not tell us how big people's homes are, whether they dare to walk home at night, or whether a fast train will take them where they need to go. This essay keeps a different set of books for 79 economies, using eight kinds of physical measures.

Data as of October 2026 · 2025 figures wherever published · adjustable weights · open data and code

Start with three puzzles.

Guyana has a GDP per person (at purchasing power parity) of about $98,000, seventh among the 79 economies here and above the United States. Yet life expectancy is 70 years, there are 19 homicides per 100,000 people a year, and electricity use per person is about one eighth of America's. Since large-scale oil production began in 2019, GDP has outrun everything else; daily life has not caught up.

Macao ranks third on GDP per person, but the average resident has about 21 square metres of home, less than a third of what a Norwegian has.

China's GDP per person is about $29,000, 50th on the list and less than a third of America's. But 94% of Chinese people feel safe walking alone at night, against 71% of Americans; China's homicide rate is a twelfth of America's; and the average Chinese travels about 960 km a year by high-speed rail and urban rail, sixteen times the American figure.

None of this is a statistical error. GDP simply measures something different from how well people live.

Where GDP goes wrong

GDP is the market value of everything newly produced in a year. That definition has four gaps, and living standards leak through each of them.

1. It counts flows, not stocks

The year a metro line is built, its cost enters GDP. For the next fifty years it carries millions of people a day and barely registers. Housing is the same: once built, a home adds nothing more, however long people live in it. Japan opened the Shinkansen in 1964; China and South Korea have built vast high-speed and metro networks over the past two decades. Much of the reason these countries live better than their GDP rank suggests is the stock they have accumulated.

2. It counts in prices, and prices differ

A bowl of noodles, a haircut or a train ticket can cost a fraction in China of what it costs in Western Europe. Purchasing power parity tries to correct for this but only partly succeeds; services are especially hard to compare. Cheap countries end up understated. Physical measures avoid the problem: a kilowatt-hour is a kilowatt-hour, a square metre is a square metre.

3. It does not care where the money goes

Oil, casinos, financial centres and multinational profits all push GDP up, but the money may stay with a few people or leave the country. Luxembourg's GDP includes the output of more than 200,000 people who commute in daily from France, Belgium and Germany, while GDP per person divides only by Luxembourg's residents.

4. Some things money cannot buy

Safety and a national rail network are public goods: however rich you are, you cannot buy them on your own. America's GDP per person is 1.6 times Japan's, yet its homicide rate is nearly 30 times higher and its people live five fewer years. High private incomes do not guarantee a society that works well.

So this essay counts differently: not what a country produces, but what its people can actually use.

Eight dimensions

We use eight dimensions, all measured in physical units: kilowatt-hours, kilograms, square metres, vehicles, kilometres, years, trips and homicides. No money-denominated data such as household consumption is used, because it cannot escape the price problem.

Electricity is the foundation of modern life: lighting, heating and cooling, cooking, washing and the internet all depend on it, and electricity use is hard to fake. We use electricity use per person, that is, domestic generation plus net imports. It includes industry, so it reflects both living conditions and industrial capacity.

Transport is half cars per person and half high-speed rail plus urban rail (metro, light rail and trams). Conventional rail is not counted; its speed and comfort are in a different league.

Housing is floor area per person. There is no international database, so we searched official statistics country by country and found figures for 32 economies.

Safety is half the share of people who feel safe walking alone at night (Gallup's worldwide survey) and half the homicide rate. Theft and robbery statistics depend on whether people report crimes and how the law defines them, so they cannot be compared across countries; homicides are hard to hide, which makes them the standard hard measure. One is a feeling, the other a fact.

Education is the tertiary gross enrolment ratio: how many young people go to university.

Missing data: when an economy lacks a dimension, that dimension takes the average score of the G20 and EU economies that have it, so the gap neither flatters nor punishes it.

How the weights were set

The default weights are 15% each for electricity, housing and health; 12.5% each for food, transport, education and safety; and 5% for the internet. No dimension exceeds 15%. Three reasons:

  • Common practice is roughly equal weights. The UN Human Development Index gives health, education and income a third each; the Legatum Prosperity Index and the Social Progress Index weight their dimensions equally. In the OECD Better Life Index, where users set their own weights, more than 100,000 users ranked health and education highest, and older users put more weight on safety and housing.
  • Dimensions that overlap with others get less. Internet and electricity scores are highly correlated (0.83), so the internet gets only 5%. Housing correlates least with the rest and adds the most independent information, so it gets 15%.
  • A check against life satisfaction. Tested against Gallup's national life-satisfaction scores (World Happiness Report, 2025), our score explains slightly more than GDP per person does (R² of about 0.47 against 0.46; 0.57 against 0.55 for the G20 and rich countries). The two explain about as much, yet rank countries very differently, which shows they measure different things. Satisfaction cannot set the weights directly because culture colours it: Latin Americans rate their lives highly despite crime, East Asians rate theirs low despite good schooling.

The results barely depend on the weights. Raise electricity to 21% and cut safety to 9%, and the same five countries still lead the G20 + rich list, China moves by one place, and no economy except Japan moves more than three places. Try the sliders below; every chart updates.

Same money, different lives

Put the score next to GDP per person: the horizontal axis is GDP per person (log scale), the vertical axis is the living-standards score, and the dashed line is the trend across all economies. Economies above the line turn the same income into better lives; for those below it, much of GDP never becomes daily life.

Note that the horizontal axis is logarithmic, so the straight trend line means each doubling of income adds about the same number of points. In other words, every extra dollar buys less improvement than the last. Among rich countries, the differences come mainly from public goods and accumulated stock, not from income.

G20 and rich countries (default weights)

  • The Nordic countries lead. Norway is 1st, Sweden 3rd, Denmark 4th and Finland 5th. They use a lot of electricity (cheap hydropower, cold winters), live long, are safe, and send many young people to university. Luxembourg is 1st on GDP per person and 2nd on living standards; Switzerland goes from 3rd to 6th.
  • China sits further above the trend line than any other economy. It is 22nd of these 27 on GDP per person and 16th on living standards. Food scores full marks; 94% of people feel safe walking alone at night and there are 0.5 homicides per 100,000, for a safety score of 96; tertiary enrolment is 77%; the average person travels about 960 km a year by high-speed and urban rail, behind only Japan and France. Floor area (about 36 m² per person) and health pull the score down.
  • South Korea (16th → 9th) and Japan (18th → 12th) rise the most: dense rail, long lives, safe streets. Japan's tertiary enrolment of 65% is the lowest among the high-income economies here.
  • The United States is 4th on GDP per person and 15th on living standards. Cars, floor area and education are near full marks, but only 71% of people feel safe walking alone at night and the homicide rate of 5.8 per 100,000 is nearly 30 times Japan's; there is little high-speed or urban rail; life expectancy trails other rich economies. Saudi Arabia (8th → 19th), the United Kingdom (14th → 18th) and Iceland (5th → 11th) also fall. Britain uses about 4,600 kWh of electricity per person, the least of the high-income economies here.
  • Russia, Argentina, Brazil, Mexico and South Africa all score low on safety. Indonesia, South Africa and India take the last three places, far behind on electricity, meat and eggs, and cars; India has about 11 m² of floor area per person and tertiary enrolment of 34%.

Across all 79 economies

  • Resource and financial economies fall furthest. Guyana, which began large-scale oil production only in 2019, is 7th on GDP per person and 72nd on living standards. Macao (3rd → 27th), Qatar (4th → 22nd), Brunei (8th → 43rd), Hong Kong (12th → 24th) and Singapore (1st → 8th) all drop. Hong Kong's floor area of about 16 m² per person is the smallest of any economy with data.
  • China is also furthest above the trend among all 79: 50th on GDP per person, 17th on living standards.
  • The populous poorer countries remain far behind. India, Nigeria and Pakistan take the last three places.

China: a life GDP undercounts

China sits further above the trend line than any other economy. For its income, its score "should" be around 62; it is 84. Where does the gap come from?

  • Public goods. Safety and rail are paid for collectively, and here China matches or beats the richest countries: 94% of people feel safe walking alone at night, and its high-speed network is longer than the rest of the world's combined. The average Chinese rides about 770 km a year by high-speed rail alone, more than the average European travels by high-speed and urban rail together.
  • Cheap, plentiful basics. Supplies of fruit, vegetables, meat and eggs per person all exceed the full-score caps. Such goods are cheap in China, and GDP measured in dollars undervalues them.
  • Investment turned into stock. China's heavy investment of the past two decades is often criticised, but much of it became the railways, metros, power grids and housing people use every day. Each entered GDP once, the year it was built, and has delivered services ever since without being counted again.

But the correction should not be overdone. China's real weaknesses are in things that take decades and cannot be rushed with money: about 36 square metres of usable floor area per person, below Europe and America; life expectancy of 78, six years short of Japan; 333 cars per 1,000 people, less than half the American level. Two measures may also flatter China. Most of its 7,460 kWh of electricity per person goes to industry; household use was only about 1,130 kWh per person in 2025. And the tertiary enrolment ratio uses UNESCO's 77%, while China's Ministry of Education reports 61.3% for 2025 on its own definition.

China's most developed region: Jiangsu-Zhejiang-Shanghai

China is too big for one average; the national figure mixes its most and least developed places. Jiangsu, Zhejiang and Shanghai together hold 177 million people and are among China's most developed regions. The table scores the region with exactly the same method as the economies and sets it beside China, the United States, the EU and Japan. Where no regional figure exists the national value is used, shown in grey: food, education, internet users, 5G coverage and safety.

With the default weights, Jiangsu-Zhejiang-Shanghai scores 88.4, 4.5 points above China as a whole (83.9). Added to the 27 G20 and rich economies, it would rank 8th, between Australia and Germany.

  • Where the region leads: rail, with about 790 km a year per person by high-speed rail and 390 km by urban rail, about 1,180 km in all, close to Japan; health, with life expectancy of about 82 years, infant mortality of 1.7 per 1,000 and a health score of 94, in the top group with Sweden and Japan; electricity, at 10,309 kWh per person, more than China as a whole, the EU or Japan, though industry takes most of it.
  • Where it lags: about 38 m² of floor area per person, a little above China's average but well below Europe and America; 314 vehicles per 1,000 people, less than half the American level, so its transport score of 72 is below Canada's (80) despite far more rail.
  • Food, education and safety use China's national figures, so the advantages there belong to the country as a whole, not to the region alone.

Notes on the regional data

    The Nordics: turning money into public goods

    Norway, Sweden, Denmark and Finland take four of the top five places. They are rich, but what sets them apart is how the money is spent: cheap hydropower means high electricity use (about 24,000 kWh per person in Norway), good healthcare means long lives, streets are safe, university is open to almost everyone, and homes are spacious (about 66 m² per person in Norway). Most of this comes from public investment built up over decades, not from what individuals can buy with their incomes.

    The United States: the richest large country, outside the top ten

    America ranks 4th on GDP per person among the G20 + rich economies and 15th on living standards. It scores near full marks on what money can buy: about 73 m² of housing per person, 800 vehicles per 1,000 people, 13,000 kWh of electricity per person. It loses on public goods: only 71% of people feel safe walking alone at night, there are 5.8 homicides per 100,000 people, and the average American travels only about 60 km a year by high-speed or urban rail. Life expectancy, at 78.9 years, is about the same as China's and five years short of Japan's; infant mortality, at 5.5 per 1,000, is higher than China's. Private wealth is abundant; public life does not lead.

    Transport: cars and rail

    The horizontal axis is vehicles per 1,000 people; the vertical axis is kilometres per person per year by high-speed and urban rail. The top right does well on both, the bottom right relies on cars, the top left on rail. The United States, Canada and Australia sit at the bottom right; Japan, France, China and South Korea near the top.

    Housing: floor area per person

    Floor area per person is the most direct measure of housing, but countries measure it differently: some report gross area, some usable area; some divide the whole housing stock (including empty homes) by the population, others count only occupied dwellings. The chart shows the 32 economies with data, all on a national (urban plus rural) basis; Singapore, Hong Kong and Macao are cities.

    Electricity: a wider gap than income

    Two sets of books

    GDP is like a company's annual income statement: it tells you how much money flowed in and out this year, not what the company has built up or how its employees actually live. Physical measures are closer to the balance sheet plus the employees' real conditions.

    That does not make GDP useless. Across these 79 economies, the living-standards score and the log of GDP per person are closely correlated (about 0.8): rich countries generally live better and poor ones worse, and GDP gets that direction right. The trouble is in particular countries. Resource and financial income, price differences, public goods and decades of accumulation can each move a country dozens of places.

    So the safest course is to read both books: GDP for the size and output of an economy, physical measures for how people actually live. The further apart they are, the more the gap deserves a question.

    Finally, these measures capture material conditions. They leave out air pollution, working hours, income distribution, freedom and rights. Add those, and some ranks would move again.

    Why clothing is not scored

    The most direct physical measure of clothing is how many kilograms of textile fibre each person consumes a year, but there are no reliable public figures by country. National "cotton consumption" counts what spinning mills use, which measures production, not what people wear: in 2008 North American mills used only 0.88 million tonnes of cotton, but counting the cotton in imported clothing, North Americans consumed 5.89 million tonnes; the Far East was the reverse. The right measure is end-use consumption, which the International Cotton Advisory Committee (ICAC) publishes only by region:

    Industrial countries consume about 30 kg of fibre per person a year, 2.7 times developing Asia and 13 times Africa; developing Asia doubled its consumption in 13 years. Clothing will join the score once reliable country figures are found.

    Limitations

    • Floor area covers only 32 economies, on different bases. Gross against usable area, housing stock against occupied homes: each can shift a figure by 10–25%. England's official per-person figure dates from 2008; Norway's comes from a 2023 self-reported survey; Denmark's is gross area including outer walls and runs high; Luxembourg's has to be derived from the 2011 census. For the 47 economies without data, housing takes the G20 and EU average ( points); their true housing may be better or worse.
    • Urban rail assumes 8 km per trip. Except for China, countries publish metro and light-rail trips, not passenger-kilometres. China's official data show about 8.1 km per trip (counting transfers), close to the assumption. Some light-rail figures cover only major cities (France only the Paris region, Switzerland only Zurich); no national tram figure was found for Japan. Buses are not counted.
    • High-speed rail years and definitions differ. China's figure is passenger-km on EMU trains, a few of which run on conventional lines. The EU sums France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Sweden and Finland; small amounts elsewhere are missing. Russia (Sapsan), Saudi Arabia (Haramain) and Indonesia (Whoosh) publish trips only, converted at full line length, an upper bound. No separate figure was found for the UK (HS1) or Hong Kong (its section of the Guangzhou line), so they count as zero.
    • Conventional rail is not counted. Commuter rail around Tokyo and other big cities carries huge numbers but is neither high-speed nor metro, so Japan's rail travel is understated.
    • Vehicle data vary in year and definition. Some figures are old, and a few (Malaysia, Brunei) may include motorcycles or many non-resident registrations. The score caps at 650 per 1,000, which limits the effect. Uruguay, Mauritius, Turkmenistan, Gabon and St Vincent have no data; their transport takes the G20 and EU average.
    • Some 5G data are old. The latest reports for Russia, Türkiye, Israel and others date from 2021, before or just after commercial 5G began, so their coverage shows as zero or very low; Argentina and Seychelles have no data. Network access takes the higher of fixed broadband and 5G, so a zero for 5G falls back on broadband.
    • Education data. Gross enrolment counts students in their own country. Most Luxembourg students study abroad, leaving a domestic ratio of 22%, so Luxembourg uses the EU average. Ratios above 100% include older students.
    • Electricity use includes industry. Iceland, Norway, Bahrain and others run energy-intensive industries such as aluminium smelting. Household use would be a purer measure but lacks consistent international data.
    • Some economies have gaps. FAO publishes no food data for Singapore, so its food score takes the G20 and EU average; the World Bank has no infant mortality for Hong Kong or Macao, so their health uses life expectancy alone. Thirteen small territories with two or more gaps are excluded.
    • Both safety measures have limits. Theft and robbery statistics depend on reporting and legal definitions and are not used. The homicide rate is hard data but misses petty crime; Indonesia's 0.3 may be under-reported; Brunei, Thailand, Gabon, Vietnam and Iran have no figure. Night-time safety comes from Gallup's 2024 survey and is coloured by public mood and culture: only 60% of Italians feel safe, despite a very low homicide rate. Macao, Qatar, Belarus and several Caribbean states were not surveyed, and the EU is weighted over 26 members (Luxembourg missing). A missing item is skipped; with both missing, safety takes the G20 and EU average.
    • Averages hide inequality. A high average does not mean most people live well.
    • More food is not better food. Each item is capped: 100 kg of fruit, 150 kg of vegetables and 72 kg of meat and eggs a year; beyond that there are no extra points.
    • Milk is not counted. Milk consumption reflects diet traditions; many East and Southeast Asians are lactose-intolerant and traditional diets contain little milk, which says nothing about how well they eat.
    • Food data are supply, not intake. FAO counts food available for consumption, including waste in shops and homes; actual intake is lower.
